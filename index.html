The story

The long way into the picture business

Bob Chinn was born on May 10, 1943, into a family whose American story already ran deep: by his own account, a great-grandfather helped build the Central Pacific Railroad. His childhood was itinerant. He grew up between Los Angeles, the Territory of Hawaii, and the desert towns of southeastern New Mexico, each move asking him to square Chinese family tradition with a different slice of mid-century America. That early education in people and places would become the observational eye his work is known for.

He studied at Santa Monica College and then the UCLA film school, graduating in 1966 from a program that counted future Doors frontman Jim Morrison among his classmates. Like most of his cohort, Chinn wanted to direct mainstream Hollywood pictures. Hollywood, as it does, declined to make room.

He wrote the first Johnny Wadd script on the back of an envelope. Two days later the picture was finished.

With a young family to feed, Chinn took the work that existed. In the late 1960s that meant clandestine loops; by the early 1970s it meant feature-length erotic films shot on real film stock and played in real theaters, during the short window later dubbed the Golden Age. Then, one afternoon in 1970, a tall, skinny young man with an unruly afro walked into his office looking for acting work. Chinn saw little potential until his partner insisted he take a second look. He hired John Holmes on the spot, scribbled a script, and shot the first Johnny Wadd in two days.

His instinct was always narrative. Where the genre traded in disconnected scenes, Chinn built a hard-boiled private eye borrowed from the noir serials and pulp paperbacks he devoured, and then brought him back, film after film, from Los Angeles Chinatown to the Hawaiian Islands to Mexico and San Francisco. It was the form's first recurring-character franchise. It changed the commercial logic of the business, and it made a star of the actor who played the part.

Chinn kept working through the 1980s and returned at the turn of the century to direct a new generation. Then he did something few of his peers managed: he sat down and wrote it all out. A crime novel first, then a two-volume memoir that is now one of the essential first-person records of the era, praised by contemporaries for its candor, its generosity toward others, and its unsparing honesty about himself.

The names

One man, a marquee full of aliases

Like many filmmakers of the era, Chinn worked under a rotating cast of pseudonyms, sometimes to protect a family life, sometimes so one name wouldn't appear five times in the same credit roll. The most colorful was Hercules Fong, the name he used as an on-screen performer in the early 1970s: in The Danish Connection (1974) he is billed as Hercules Fong, playing the fighter in white. Behind the camera he signed as Daniel Hussong, Harry Who, and a dozen others.

The career

A working life, by the year

  1. 1943

    Born into a moving family

    Born to Chinese American parents; raised in Los Angeles, the Territory of Hawaii, and southeastern New Mexico.

  2. 1966

    Out of UCLA

    Graduates from the UCLA film school, a classmate of Jim Morrison, intending to direct for Hollywood.

  3. late 1960s

    Into the trade

    Begins shooting short loops to support his family; learns to make a picture fast, cheap, and watchable.

  4. 1970–71

    Johnny Wadd

    Hires John Holmes, writes the script on an envelope, and finishes the first film in two days. The audience asks for a sequel immediately.

  5. 1971–78

    The nine-film run

    Directs the complete original Johnny Wadd cycle, from Flesh of the Lotus to Blonde Fire, spanning Chinatown, Hawaii, Mexico, and San Francisco.

  6. 1974

    Hercules Fong on screen

    Appears as a performer under his most memorable alias in The Danish Connection.

  7. 1977–80

    Peak output

    Helms a string of theatrical features including The Jade Pussycat, Candy Stripers, Hot & Saucy Pizza Girls, and Prisoner of Paradise, working with stars like Rene Bond, Desiree Cousteau, and Jesie St. James.

  8. 1980s–90s

    The working director

    Continues directing through the video era, including Baby Cakes, All the Way In, and late-1990s features.

  9. 1997–99

    Recognized

    Boogie Nights borrows heavily from his Johnny Wadd years; he is interviewed at length for the documentary Wadd: The Life & Times of John C. Holmes.

  10. 2001–02

    The revival

    Returns to direct a new four-film Johnny Wadd series with Joel Lawrence in the role.

  11. 2011

    The novelist

    Publishes Flesh of the Lotus: A Johnny Wadd Novel, his debut as a crime writer, with BearManor Media.

  12. 2013

    On the record

    Sits for a 98-minute oral-history interview with The Rialto Report, one of the definitive accounts of the West Coast scene.

  13. 2016–17

    The memoirist

    Releases The Other Side of Paradise: first as a two-volume Author's Uncut Edition, then as a single complete edition from Rare Bird Books.

The books

Then he wrote it all down

Chinn read pulp detective novels by the dozen, and the Johnny Wadd films were always more travelogue-thriller than most of their neighbors. Eight years after his last feature, he tried the page. The result was a crime novel, then a memoir that has become one of the most valuable first-person accounts of the Golden Age: the family history, the Hawaii years, the fight against censorship, the business, the wives and children he admits he didn't always do right by, and John Holmes in full, from boyish early enthusiasm to the man his fame made of him.

The legacy

Beyond the screen

The Boogie Nights connection

Chinn is widely cited as the primary real-life basis for Burt Reynolds' director Jack Horner in Paul Thomas Anderson's Boogie Nights (1997), which draws heavily on his years making the Johnny Wadd films with Holmes; some scenes are lifted nearly intact from his pictures.

The franchise idea

By building a recurring character, a continuing story, and far-flung locations into adult features, Chinn changed how the genre told and sold its stories. Every series that followed owes him the template.

Telling the real story

He was interviewed extensively for Wadd: The Life & Times of John C. Holmes (1999), the definitive documentary about the star he discovered, and remains the most reliable witness to that partnership.

The chronicler

Between the novel, the memoir, and the oral histories he has given, Chinn turned himself into the era's historian, a self-effacing, walking encyclopedia of the West Coast scene across four decades.
